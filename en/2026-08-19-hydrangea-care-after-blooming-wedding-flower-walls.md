If you’re planning a wedding and dreaming of lush hydrangea arrangements, you might be growing your own flowers to save on costs. But hydrangeas can be tricky—especially when it comes to pruning. Prune at the wrong time, and you could lose next year’s blooms entirely. That’s a heartbreak no DIY bride wants.

In this guide, we’ll walk you through how to care for your hydrangeas after they finish flowering, so you can protect next year’s display. And if you’re considering a wedding flower wall backdrop, we’ll also share how silk flowers can give you that lush look without the pruning stress.

## Why Timing Matters for Hydrangea Pruning

Hydrangeas fall into two main categories: those that bloom on old wood and those that bloom on new wood. Old-wood hydrangeas, like bigleaf (Hydrangea macrophylla) and oakleaf (Hydrangea quercifolia), form their flower buds during late summer and fall, right after they finish blooming. If you prune them too late in the season, you’ll cut off next year’s buds.

For wedding planners and DIY brides, this means if you’re growing hydrangeas for your own wedding flower wall backdrop, you need to be extra careful. The safest time to prune old-wood hydrangeas is shortly after they finish flowering—usually by August. If you miss that window, it’s better to skip pruning altogether than risk losing the blooms.

## How to Identify Old-Wood Hydrangeas

Before you grab your pruning shears, take a close look at your plant. Old-wood hydrangeas have specific characteristics:

- **Bigleaf hydrangeas** (mophead and lacecap) have large, rounded or flat flower clusters.
- **Oakleaf hydrangeas** have distinctive oak-shaped leaves and cone-shaped blooms.
- **Mountain hydrangeas** resemble bigleaf but are smaller and often have lacecap flowers.

If you’re unsure, a good rule of thumb is to wait until after flowering and then prune only dead or damaged stems. This conservative approach keeps your plant healthy without risking next year’s buds.

## What to Do If You Missed the Pruning Window

If it’s already late summer or fall and you haven’t pruned, don’t panic. The best move is to leave the plant alone. Removing only the spent flower heads is okay, but avoid cutting back healthy stems. This way, you’ll preserve the buds that are already forming.

## Practical Tips for Protecting Your Hydrangeas

Here are some quick tips to keep your hydrangeas thriving:

- **Prune old-wood varieties immediately after flowering** (by August at the latest).
- **For reblooming varieties** (like certain bigleaf and mountain hydrangeas), you have more flexibility—they can flower on both old and new wood.
- **If in doubt, don’t prune.** A healthy, well-shaped hydrangea doesn’t need annual pruning.
- **Mulch around the base** to protect roots during winter.
- **Water deeply** during dry spells, especially in late summer when buds are forming.

By following these steps, you’ll ensure a stunning display next year—whether you’re using fresh blooms or silk alternatives.
